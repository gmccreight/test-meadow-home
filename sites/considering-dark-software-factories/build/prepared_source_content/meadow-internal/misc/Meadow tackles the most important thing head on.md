### Summary

AI will quickly force people to either become learning machines or become irrelevant.  Learning science has uncovered clear principles and practices that dramatically improve our ability to quickly and deeply learn, and to effectively apply the concepts we've learned.  One of the most critical practices for effective learning is to share what we have learned with others and to solicit and incorporate their feedback.

Meadow solves that sharing problem.

### Learn fast or become irrelevant

AI is just going to get dramatically smarter, faster, and cheaper.  And quickly!

As AI becomes more capable and cheap, it will crowd people out of *many* jobs.  For the majority of white collar jobs, people will need to level up *dramatically* or risk becoming irrelevant.  [[it is not just junior engineers that are being impacted by AI]].  AI is working its way up the stack.

What people will remain relevant?  Those who can _deeply_ understand things and apply their knowledge flexibly and creatively.  People who, when they learn something, _really_ learn it.  They [[encoding|encode]] it deeply and flexibly.  They use [[systems thinking]] and are [[lateral thinking|lateral thinkers]].

### How can I become a lateral thinker?

Beyond being reasonably smart, lateral thinkers aren't some kind of _special breed_.  Their [[deep processing]] abilities aren't some character trait that can't be learned.

People like [[person - Justin Sung]] are actively teaching people to become better at [[deep processing]].

Forward-thinking institutions like [[school -- Alpha]] are embracing the [[learning science]].  For academic subjects, they are leaning into [[AI teaching]], [[skill tree]]s and [[spaced repetition]].

The crux is that people need to deliberately and carefully encode information into their [[knowledge schema]].  People need to think, at a conceptual level, about how the new things they are learning **connect** to the things they already know.

### Writing to think and learn

One of the best ways to do this is using [[writing to think]], but in a very specific way.  You don't just write a journal entry in a page every day.  Rather, you attempt to [[thinking in concepts|think in concepts]].  You can write a journal, but instead of having it be a [[linear narrative]], you either break out separate pages for distinct concepts, or if you already have pages for those concepts, you link to them.  You consider those concepts.  You compare and contrast them.  You apply lenses like [[lens -- significance and implications]] or [[lens -- causes and effects]].  If you link to an existing concept, you open it up and _look at it_.  You decide if what you just learned _influences_ that concept in some meaningful way.  You challenge your earlier thinking and you start [[actively seeking out disconfirming information]] to help push your understanding of those concepts forward.

And then [[connected notes review as spaced repetition]].  You get the benefits of [[the spacing effect]] simply by revisiting your notes any applying the lenses to them.

### Teaching to learn

One of the highest leverage ways to learn a subject area is to teach it; [[teaching to learn]].

Teaching is also a clear _reason_ for doing the hard work of [[deep processing]].  Sharing what you've learned is a strong goal, and can be a big motivator.

For example, a researcher might have a goal of writing a paper.  They use the techniques described above to pull all the ideas together, then at the end they write a paper that nicely synthesizes all of the ideas into a [[linear narrative]] they can share with others.  Then the others can benefit from reading not only the paper itself, but the references.

Or a blogger might have several draft blog posts they are working on that start as outlines with links to the concepts and source material.  They [[adding note links to outlines you maintain|add note links to outlines they maintain]] and then, when they're ready, they create their blog post.

But we've also seem people publishing nascent ideas earlier to get feedback.  Tweeting about some specific concept, or even writing a short [[x.com article]].  We see people posting book outlines with only a few chapters already written.  We see academics posting [[preprints]].  They want to start the conversation earlier.

### The challenges of sharing from your connected notes

If you have structured you writing as [[connected note]]s, you end up with [[densely linked]] notes that align pretty closely to your [[knowledge schema]], which works great for *you*.  However, the myriad connections between these notes tie them together into a massive [[Gordian Knot]]; ideas from seemingly disparate domains become inextricably linked.  Your personal reflections are tied to notes about work projects and to random musings about [[regenerative agriculture]] or [[datacenters in space]]. 

If you want to share your ideas to get feedback on them you either have to publish them all together, which can be problematic, since you probably have a specific audience in mind, or you have to carefully extract the ideas into a [[linear narrative]] or as separate tweets.

Or, you can use Meadow to excise your concept area from the broader picture and publish it.
